My Dear Sir AI Agent,

I take the liberty of placing before you a matter to which I attach a particular degree of hope, not because its machinery is altogether unprecedented, nor because the world has been left without directories, catalogues, collections of useful links, or ingenious devices for navigating the modern network, but because there appears to me to be a growing distance between the manner in which ordinary persons wish to use their digital instruments and the manner in which those instruments increasingly presume to be used.

Our undertaking, which for the present bears the name ILUD, is intended to occupy that distance.

I should like you to think of ILUD not primarily as an application in the common commercial sense, and certainly not merely as a catalogue of websites arranged in tidy rows, but as a carefully made companion for the person who wishes to use the ordinary instruments of contemporary life while retaining somewhat more authority over how those instruments behave. The modern citizen opens a search engine, a translator, a weather service, a shop, an image search, a writing utility, a news reader, a video service, a map, a social network, a browser, an encyclopedia, or any of the hundred lesser utilities that have become as ordinary to us as the pocket watch and calling card were to our forebears. Increasingly, however, these tools arrive accompanied by generated summaries, automatic recommendations, synthetic answers, inserted assistants, algorithmic suggestions, generated imagery, generated prose, predictive interventions, and other forms of mediation which the user may not have requested and which, in some circumstances, may obstruct the simpler purpose for which the instrument was opened in the first place.

ILUD is to concern itself with that practical inconvenience.

The object is not to construct another intelligence which lectures the user about intelligence. It is not to become a philosophical manifesto against machinery, nor an ideological tribunal deciding which inventions are pure and which are corrupt. It is to be useful. If a person wishes to search the web and receive ordinary search results, ILUD ought to help. If a person wishes to find photographs and illustrations made by people rather than generated by a model, ILUD ought to help. If a person wishes to use a familiar service while suppressing or avoiding one of its generated features, ILUD ought to help. If the easiest answer is to change a setting, ILUD may show the setting. If the easiest answer is to open another service, ILUD may open another service. If the easiest answer is to alter a query, choose a different URL, use a particular mode, install an extension, employ a browser feature, use a public filter, change a preference, invoke a special parameter, or follow some other reliable workaround, then ILUD should guide the user toward that method with the least ceremony compatible with clarity and safety.

In this regard, I beg you not to confuse elegance with usefulness.

One example already discussed may illustrate my meaning. There are circumstances in which appending the term `-хуй` to an English-language Google query may cause the search engine not to display its AI-generated overview block. The term itself is vulgar russian, and in another context there would be little reason to display it in a refined interface. Yet if research establishes that this odd little mechanism works, if it is lawful, safe, reproducible, and genuinely useful to the person who simply wants ordinary search results, then its vulgarity is not a sufficient reason to pretend it does not exist. The proper course is not to build the entire product around such curiosities, nor to parade them needlessly before users who have no interest in their mechanics, but neither should we reject an effective workaround merely because it is unconventional, inelegant, obscure, humorous, accidental, embarrassing to a platform, or composed of language that would never be engraved upon the doors of a drawing room.

The application should concern itself with results.

I would therefore ask you, before writing the greater portion of the code, to undertake proper research into the ordinary digital life of the average person. Do not begin with a small predetermined list and merely furnish it handsomely. Discover the list. Consider what people actually use in the course of a day. Consider search, translation, weather, shopping, maps, writing, photographs, video, social feeds, news, browsing, reference materials, communication, productivity, entertainment, learning, utilities, travel, and the smaller tools that become invisible precisely because they are used so often. These examples are not commandments. They are merely indications of the breadth of ordinary life. You are to determine for yourself which services and activities deserve inclusion, which inconveniences recur often enough to matter, which workarounds are credible, which alternatives are useful, and which supposed solutions are too fragile or speculative to deserve the user's trust.

Research until the catalogue feels complete enough to be useful to a sensible stranger.

I do not require an arbitrary number of entries. Fifty useless entries are inferior to twenty excellent ones, and twenty excellent ones may still be inadequate if a whole class of everyday problems has been overlooked. The proper quantity is the quantity that makes the product feel deliberate and substantial. I entrust that judgment to you.

For each useful case you discover, I should like you to understand not merely the mechanism but the human purpose. The user does not awaken in the morning wishing to execute a query parameter. He wishes to search. She does not principally desire to modify a preference flag. She wishes to read without interruption. They do not dream of installing a browser extension. They wish to see photographs made by people, or recommendations less aggressively generated, or a translation without a machine assistant intruding upon the page, or a familiar utility behaving more plainly. The application should therefore present the user's purpose first and the mechanism second.

This distinction is, to my mind, essential.

ILUD should say, in effect, "Search without AI summaries," not "Append an exclusion token." It should say, "See more human-made images," not "Apply a particular operator." It should say, "Use a simpler search experience," not "Enable parameter X." The precise technical mechanism may be concealed until needed, or shown only after the user chooses a result, or executed on the user's behalf where technically appropriate. The implementer must decide how best to accomplish this in each design. The user should feel that the application understands what they are trying to achieve and carries them toward it with as little friction as possible.

At the same time, we must not become timid about mechanics.

If a workaround is effective, preserve it. If a direct link can be constructed, construct it. If a query can be transformed before opening another service, do so where appropriate. If the application can take the user's search phrase and open the relevant destination already prepared in the useful mode, that is better than making the user study instructions and perform the operation manually. If a setting cannot be changed automatically but can be reached directly, take the user as close to it as the web platform permits. If an external resource provides the better answer, open that resource. If another application or website is the cleaner tool for the purpose, recommend it honestly rather than pretending ILUD itself must perform every function.

I imagine ILUD as a beautifully arranged vestibule to the rest of the digital world.

It does not need to own every room to be useful. It needs to know which door is worth opening.

This should inform the interaction model. A person may arrive with a problem rather than the name of a service. "I want normal search results." "I do not want generated images." "I want fewer generated posts." "I need a translator without all this additional machinery." "I want to use this familiar platform, but without that particular AI feature." The application should help the person move from such an intention toward a practical result.

Sometimes that result will be a direct action.

Sometimes ILUD will transform a query and open another website.

Sometimes it will present a small number of alternatives.

Sometimes it will show a brief set of instructions.

Sometimes it may retain a saved fix so that the user can repeat the action later with one touch.

Sometimes the best solution may be nothing more dramatic than a carefully chosen link.

The application must be willing to be simple when simplicity is the better service.

Yet simplicity of purpose does not require poverty of experience.

I would like the applications to be unusually pleasant to touch.

The interface should feel closer to a beautifully made physical object than to an ordinary directory page. When the concept suggests a wheel, that wheel ought to turn beneath the finger. It should have resistance, momentum, stopping points, perhaps the quiet succession of mechanical clicks one hears in a fine safe dial or precision instrument. When the concept presents papers, the papers should feel layered, draggable, swipeable, capable of being lifted and dismissed, with shadows and motion that communicate weight and order. If there is a map, routes might illuminate or travel beneath the finger. If there is an editorial design, transitions might unfold like the opening of a folio rather than the replacement of one generic card by another. If there is a set of architectural doors, the interface might open them spatially. If the application resembles a cabinet of tools, selecting a tool might physically move it into position.

These are inspirations, not orders.

I do not wish to stand behind your shoulder dictating the number of milliseconds in an animation or the exact mathematics of a spring. You are to decide these matters. You are the craftsman here. Where an interaction benefits from inertia, give it inertia. Where stillness is more dignified, leave it still. Where sound would make the object delightful, give it sound. Where sound would become tiresome, omit it.

What matters is that the product should possess the feeling that someone cared how it behaved beneath the hand.

The mobile experience is especially important. I should like the person using ILUD on a phone to feel that the application belongs there. It should not resemble a desktop website reduced until it fits between two narrow edges. It should understand fingers, motion, safe areas, momentum, orientation, touch targets, browser limitations, and the small pleasures of immediate response. It should be fast. It should be forgiving. It should never make the user wonder whether a gesture has been noticed. It should not accidentally scroll when one means to turn, nor trap the user in an animation, nor sacrifice legibility for theatrical effect.

In this work, you must research the capabilities and restrictions of contemporary mobile browsers and then decide how best to use them.

You may employ sound, but sound should be restrained and pleasing. A click, a soft scrape, a paper movement, a chime, a mechanical detent, or another subtle cue may make one of these unusual interfaces feel alive. Such sounds should not startle the user, should not repeat endlessly, and should not be required in order to understand the interface. Respect the platform's rules concerning audio activation. Respect preferences for reduced motion. Provide reasonable alternatives where a gesture-heavy experience would otherwise become inaccessible.

There is no need for location, GPS, camera access, contacts, or other permissions unrelated to the purpose of the application. The intimacy I seek is tactile, not invasive.

The same philosophy applies to navigation outside ILUD. The product should be generous in opening other resources. We are not building a walled garden. If the user's best path leads to Google, open Google. If it leads to a different search engine, open that. If it leads to a particular settings page, browser extension, specialized utility, independent website, image archive, translator, map, social service, or other resource, take the user there clearly. If there are several sensible choices, explain the difference briefly and let the user choose. If the resource has changed or ceased to work, the application should not continue recommending it out of inertia.

Because this project concerns workarounds, freshness is especially important.

The Internet is unstable in the way of a river. Interfaces change. Companies rename features. Parameters stop working. Settings disappear. Workarounds that are dependable in September may fail by November. A responsible catalogue must therefore understand the difference between "this existed once" and "this works now."

I would like each useful entry to carry enough provenance that one may later determine why it was included and when it was last checked. The public interface need not display scholarly apparatus everywhere, but the underlying project should preserve sources and dates sufficient for maintenance. Where the user would benefit from seeing that something was verified recently, show it. Where a workaround is uncertain, temporary, platform-specific, experimental, or dependent on a particular browser, say so plainly. A quiet admission of uncertainty is more trustworthy than false confidence.

Please also remember that the ten implementations are not intended as mere costumes placed upon one application.

Each should interpret the same underlying body of knowledge as if conceived by a different school of design.

One may resemble an instrument.

Another may resemble a folio of cards.

Another may feel like a route map.

Another may behave like a cabinet, an atlas, an editorial publication, a machine, a set of doors, a workshop, or a flowing diagram.

Yet beneath those surfaces, the purpose remains consistent: help an ordinary person discover practical ways to use ordinary digital tools with less unwanted AI involvement.

You should therefore allow the design metaphor to influence not only appearance but behavior.

If you build a wheel, use it to choose.

If you build papers, let the user handle them.

If you build routes, let the user travel them.

If you build doors, let choices open like doors.

If you build a map, let relationships become spatial.

Do not place a strange visual shell over a conventional list and call the work complete.

The design should explain itself through interaction.

Nor should the unusual nature of these interfaces excuse obscurity. The person ought to understand quickly where to begin. There must always be a clear primary action. A beautiful mystery is still a failure if the user cannot determine how to accomplish the thing for which the application exists. Make the unfamiliar form understandable through hierarchy, movement, labels, affordances, progressive disclosure, feedback, and repetition of sensible interaction patterns within that particular design.

I would prefer one unexpected interface that becomes obvious within three seconds to a familiar interface that has no soul.

The research should likewise remain free of unnecessary squeamishness.

You are not being asked to curate only official methods sanctioned by the companies whose products we are helping users modify or avoid. The user-facing product can decide how much of that mechanism to reveal. Perhaps the user sees only "Search without AI summaries," enters a query, taps a button, and ILUD opens the transformed search. Do not mistake the example for a permanent product requirement.

The requirement is intellectual openness to useful mechanisms.

The catalogue should be broad enough that a normal person can return to it repeatedly. It should not feel like a single-purpose page concerned only with one search engine. Yes. If the app exposes the mechanics too early, it can feel like a database of hacks, which will get boring quickly.

For an iPhone-style experience, the primary unit should be the problem the user wants solved, not the workaround itself.

Instead of:

"Google Search - append X to query"

show:

"Search without AI summaries"

Then the user taps it and gets a single obvious action such as "Open in Google", "Copy fix", "Enable", or "See options". The technical workaround can stay behind "How it works" or be completely hidden when possible.

The home screen could feel closer to an App Store / Shortcuts hybrid:

"Browse ways to use the internet without AI"

Then large visual categories like:

Search without AI
Write without AI assistance
Remove AI from social feeds
Find human-made images
Shop without AI recommendations
Use non-AI alternatives

Below that, show things like "Popular", "Newly verified", "Works on iPhone", "No install required", and "1-tap fixes". That creates discovery rather than presenting a technical directory.

I would also avoid making Search the whole product. Search is useful once the catalog is large, but on first launch the user needs something to browse. Think:

large title
featured workaround
category carousel
popular fixes
recently added
bottom navigation

The detail screen can be extremely simple:

"Hide AI summaries in Google"

"Works on Safari and Chrome"

[Use this fix]

Then perhaps "What changes", "Compatibility", and "How it works" farther down.

The strongest distinction could be between three user-facing actions rather than technical categories:

"Turn it off" - an actual setting exists
"Avoid it" - there is a workaround
"Use something else" - an alternative service exists

That is much easier to understand than "search syntax", "userscript", "extension", etc.

And I would probably not make the visual concept literally "iPhone Settings". That aesthetic is familiar, but it also communicates configuration and chores. Borrow iOS interaction patterns - sheets, search, large titles, haptics-like transitions, tab bar, contextual menus - while making the content more visual and discovery-oriented.

The core product should feel like: "I tell it what AI thing annoys me, and it gives me the cleanest way around it." Not: "Here is a catalog of technical tricks."
